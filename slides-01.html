Listening Part 1 · Photographs

Before we open the book, let's work out what this part is really testing.

What happens

You see a photo. You hear four statements.

You see

One photograph. That is all.

You hear

Four statements — (A) (B) (C) (D) — read once.

You read

Nothing. The statements are not printed anywhere.

On today's test this is the shortest part — about six photos — so every single mark counts.

Why people lose marks

All four sentences sound reasonable.

Three of them describe something that is nearly in the picture. They are wrong by one word.

Wrong noun

You see a laptop. You hear “…is reading a menu.”

Wrong verb

She is typing. You hear “…is writing a letter.”

So the whole part comes down to two words: the noun and the verb.

Try it

A woman is typing on a laptop in a café.

AThe woman is writing a letter.wrong verb
BThe woman is reading a menu.wrong noun
CThe woman is using a computer.correct
DThe women are sitting together.wrong number

Notice (C) never says laptop or typing. The right answer is often the most general one.

The tactic

Do your thinking before the audio starts.

  • Look at the photo. Who or what is in it?
  • Say nouns to yourself: people, screen, table…
  • Say verbs: sitting, holding, looking…
  • Now listen — and wait to hear your words.

Your turn — out loud

Three nouns, three verbs. Ten seconds.

Picture this: two men in suits at a desk, papers between them, one is pointing at a document.

Nouns: men, suits, desk, papers, document.
Verbs: sitting, pointing, looking, discussing, reading.

That is the whole skill. You just did Part 1.

Now the book

Brainstorm the vocabulary, predict the statements, then a mini-test at real test speed.

Open the lesson →
Say: Four days a week, two listening and two reading. Today is the first listening part. Keep it quick — the deck is the lead-in, not the lesson.
Ask first: “Has anyone seen a TOEIC photo question?” Stress that nothing is printed — this surprises people who have only done reading tests.
Press → to reveal the two boxes, then the line. Point out that a distractor is usually only one word wrong, which is why guessing from the general topic fails.
Reveal one option at a time. Ask the class to reject A, B and D before you show the tags. The last line is the real insight — the safest answer is often the vaguest one.
Reveal one step at a time and have them repeat each step back. This is exactly the routine they will use in Activity 1 and 3.
Do this orally, no writing. Give them ten seconds, take answers round the group, then reveal. If they are slow, do one more of your own before moving on.
